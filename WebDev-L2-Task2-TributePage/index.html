01 / THE PERSON

Curiosity was her
starting point.

Before she became a name associated with space, she was a student fascinated by flight—and determined to learn how it worked.

Born in Karnal, Haryana, Kalpana Chawla grew up looking toward a world that extended far beyond the familiar. She studied aeronautical engineering at Punjab Engineering College in Chandigarh, graduating in 1982, then moved to the United States for advanced studies in aerospace engineering.

She earned a master's degree from the University of Texas at Arlington in 1984 and a doctorate from the University of Colorado Boulder in 1988. Her work in computational fluid dynamics and aircraft aerodynamics built the scientific foundation for a career that would eventually take her into orbit.

In 1994, NASA selected Chawla for its astronaut corps. She completed astronaut training and became a mission specialist, contributing technical expertise and the careful work required to operate in space.

“It's easy for me to be motivated and inspired by seeing somebody who just goes all out to do something.”— Kalpana Chawla · NASA preflight interview, STS-107

Her story is not simply about reaching space. It is about years of study, persistence, teamwork, and practical skill behind an extraordinary ambition.

03 / THE JOURNEY

From a classroom
to the cosmos.

A timeline of study, research, flight, and the mission that became part of spaceflight history.

1962

A beginning in Karnal

Kalpana Chawla was born on 17 March 1962 in Karnal, Haryana, India.

1982

Engineering takes flight

She completed a bachelor's degree in aeronautical engineering at Punjab Engineering College in Chandigarh.

1984–1988

Deeper into aerospace

She earned a master's degree at the University of Texas at Arlington, followed by a doctorate at the University of Colorado Boulder.

1988–1994

Research before orbit

Her work included computational fluid dynamics and aerodynamic optimisation. In 1994, NASA selected her as an astronaut candidate.

1997

First journey into space · STS-87

On Space Shuttle Columbia, she served as mission specialist and prime robotic arm operator during a mission studying microgravity and the Sun's outer atmosphere.

2003

A science mission · STS-107

Columbia launched on 16 January for a dedicated research mission. The crew worked in shifts to carry out a broad programme of scientific experiments.

01 FEB 2003

Remembering Columbia

During re-entry, Space Shuttle Columbia was lost with all seven crew members. Chawla's life ended with the mission, but her work and the inspiration she offered continue to be remembered.

04 / THE WORK

More than a journey.
A mission of discovery.

Spaceflight is not only a feat of courage. It is engineering, collective effort, and research that helps us understand our world.

STS-87 · 1997

Learning in microgravity

Chawla's first spaceflight supported experiments examining how weightlessness affects physical processes. She also served as the prime robotic arm operator.

STS-107 · 2003

A laboratory in orbit

The second mission was dedicated to science, with investigations spanning life sciences, physical sciences, Earth observations, and technology.

THE ENGINEER

Precision behind the wonder

Her background in aerospace engineering and computational fluid dynamics reflects the technical work behind flight: modelling, testing, and understanding how air moves around aircraft.

THE TEAM

Discovery is shared

Both missions depended on teamwork. Astronauts, engineers, researchers, and mission controllers contributed to an effort no single person could accomplish alone.

05 / WHAT REMAINS

Her journey ended.
The questions did not.

Kalpana Chawla's life is remembered not because tragedy defines her, but because she pursued knowledge with commitment and helped make space exploration more tangible to people who had never imagined themselves there. Her story continues to invite young people—especially girls—to see science and engineering as places where they belong.

She was one member of the seven-person Columbia crew. Remembering her also means remembering her crewmates, their work, and the importance of learning from the risks of human spaceflight.
